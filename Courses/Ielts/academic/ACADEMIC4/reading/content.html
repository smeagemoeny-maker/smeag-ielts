Managing Risk

A. Among a wide range of fields, the word 'risk' entails the probability of loss. As a concept, it is useful in areas as diverse as military conflict, public health, business, and finance. It is a very broad term that can refer to such things as the handling of radioactive materials or to the construction safety of bridges and other infrastructure. One particular scope of risk involves institutions' understanding about all new projects that have the potential to bring in more profit, but also engender some risk; at the very least, there is the prospect of losing the initial outlay without having produced any tangible results. Research over the past few decades has created frameworks that help to identify and mitigate risks. This has made the task of venturing into new areas less frightening for companies when they rationally evaluate the risks they take on in an effort to expand their operations and income.

B. Any change in the way a company works, like implementing a new Internet technology system or a franchising scheme, requires understanding the risk at hand. Identification is the first step in any decision-making process. Identifying risk can be broken down into several constituent parts depending on the nature of the problem and the type of institution conducting the analysis. Sources of risk are identified in order to formulate and deploy potential mitigating strategies.

C. For example, a software engineering project requires numerous man-hours and high labor costs. An internal source of risk for that particular project is the possibility that parts of the software coded by different teams might be incompatible, resulting in a product that requires more capital input than planned. For the same software project, one source of exogenous risk, as opposed to endogenous, might be a downturn in the national economy that dries up demand for the final software product. Risk identification strategies may also include simulations, analysis of different market scenarios, or, for particular areas, checking a common database of known risks that have been identified.

D. Once potential risks have been identified, both the probability of the problem actually occurring and how much the problem would cost if it did materialize need to be calculated. Multiplying the two numbers together can give a value, which can then be compared to the values generated by all other identified risks. This kind of risk assessment is useful for helping corporations prioritize risks that need to be mitigated, though there are some difficulties inherent in the methodology.

E. The value of a lost shipment of cargo is easily quantifiable but the probability of a major weather event that causes the loss is not. Also, since the probability of the event occurring and the cost of that event are not easily determined, it is difficult to prioritize between: a) events that have a very high cost, but relatively low chance of occurring; and b) events that have minimal costs, but will almost certainly occur. With only finite amounts of capital, material, and time, deciding which risk to focus on becomes very important.

F. After agreeing on which risks should be mitigated, there are several options that groups can choose from. Dr. Merlin Dorfman, a software engineer, identifies four types of treatment for potential risks: Avoidance, Reduction, Transfer, and Retention. All four strategies for approaching risks have benefits and drawbacks. Avoidance dictates not engaging in the particular activity that carries the risk, the cost of which is forgoing any potential profit. Reduction of risk is intuitively very familiar to most people and involves the expenditure of resources to reduce costs in the event of a loss. Installing sprinkler systems in a building to reduce damage sustained in a fire is one common example. Transfer is where another party takes on the risk, with one typical method being insurance. Retention simply means accepting the risk and covering it with available resources. This includes risks where losses are so small that addressing them would cost more money than is lost, and also risks that are so catastrophic and improbable as to be uninsurable.

G. In an ever-changing world, dealing with the unexpected creates stronger institutions. A comprehensive and rigorous risk strategy is part of that plan, especially when large amounts of capital and livelihoods are on the line.

Human Diaspora

A. The question of exactly how the Earth became populated by humanity beggars our everyday notion of self. The government and nation state under which people live are usually quite familiar in such a way that it is difficult to imagine a time when no such social structures existed. That there was a pre-human time on the planet is not in dispute. Exactly how humans came to imprint their own mental and cultural architecture on the physical landscape across the world is quite important. The discourse that a single person engages in with himself or herself, identity. Similarly, the story of the human diaspora across the face of the planet, as told by Western science, supplements the rich and varied ways that people across the world see themselves.

B. In the field of paleoanthropology (study of human origins) the great majority of evidence now points to a single origin hypothesis out of Africa. In 1871, Charles Darwin prefigured this development in his book Descent of Man when he conjectured it was “somewhat … probable that our early progenitors lived on the African continent.” This went against another view held in Western science that Europe was the 'natural' birthplace of all humankind. With modern technology, the origin of modern humans has been confirmed. The time and circumstances under which those first peoples left their homes and settled the entire planet have also been roughly sketched.

C. Climate change was the driving factor that drove both the evolution of proto-hominid species and also migration out of the continent. Around two million years ago, climate factors changed the environment such that previously tree-dwelling species were forced upright onto the ground and able to use tools. These new selection pressures resulted in a species able to walk upright and able to use tools. The climate changes also allowed early hominids to migrate through the usually arid Sahara and Middle East, populating the rest of the Eurasian landmass, but not Australia nor the Americas, for the next one million years.

D. The next part of the human story comes from a variety of sources including the fossil record, which can trace where human species went in the world and at what time; two types of genetic evidence, one of which is mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and the other being Y-chromosome DNA; and geologic cores findings that give insights into weather patterns millennia ago; archaeological findings that indicate what tools early humans used. Scientists have taken core samples from one of the world's deepest lakes, Lake Malawi, in southeastern Africa in order to determine climatic conditions in previous geological eras. Sediments from around 100,000 years ago indicate the occurrence of a massive 'megadrought' in the region when the level of Lake Malawi dropped by at least 600 meters. All humans currently inhabiting the African Earth today are descended from a small band, perhaps numbering just about 2000 people, who survived that continent wide devastation. Then, starting about 70,000 years ago, more favorable wet and humid weather conditions allowed the desendants of those survivors to cross into Eurasia and, eventually, to all other major landmasses, excluding Antarctia.

E. The genetic evidence from which scientists developed this model is based on the characteristics of a special kind of DNA in cells. Mitochondria are the power stations of the body since they produce most of the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) used to create chemical energy for cellular processes. Mitochondria are also notable for containing DNA that is outside the cell nucleus. This is important because the mitochondria present in cells that are involved in reproduction and fertilization come exclusively from the egg, or the mother's side. This mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutates at a higher rate than nuclear DNA. By measuring the differences in samples from enough populations, geneticists can determine a rough timeline of when humans settled Australia arrived where they did, corresponding with the exit out of Africa. In the rest of Eurasia, previous human species had already established themselves but were eventually displaced by Homo sapiens sapiens.

F. Paleoanthropological studies are useful for highlighting how closely universal cultural characteristics are present in all groups of human beings. Anthropologists have noticed certain universal superficial characteristics such as skin pigmentation, have only recently been expressed. Nina Jablonski and George Chaplin of the California Academy of Sciences state that "coloration in humans is adaptive and labile. Skin pigmentation levels have changed more than once in human evolution," giving weight to the argument that arbitrary categorization is not, in fact, part of the real human story here on Earth.

World Capital Flows

A. The 1990s oversaw again a transformation in the global economy that completely changed the way corporations and developing countries were financed. Culminating in what was perhaps the worst international financial crisis since the 1930s, the liberalization of capital across international borders proved to be a double-edged sword. Various institutions now have greater access to money markets for the purpose of funding expansion and growth, but now also face a higher risk environment. Whether it is to protect against dubious financial arrangements or against purposeful manipulation of capital flows, the modern global economy requires certain safeguards and regulations so that all countries with access to investment can climb the ladder of economic growth and not be subject to undue risks caused by liquidity crises and speculative panics.

B. Banking and finance have a history that has long been intertwined with the expansion of civilization and the consolidation of state resources. The earliest locations where capital could be stored and lent developed in the Middle East thousands of years before the Common Era. By the 1600s, most every modern financial instrument had been developed and deployed to help expand the resource gathering capabilities of European nations.

C. The current global financial architecture was implemented after the global conflicts of the 1930s and 40s. Ostensibly, such measures were needed to finance the vast reconstruction of industrialized countries and to bind up nations in a way that such a war would not be possible again. The ideological clash between communist revolutionaries and capitalist ideologues limited the ability of capital, along with goods/services and labor, to cross national borders.

D. The particular form of communism expounded by the Soviets was not conducive to the rule of law and private property. No institution would seek to make an investment where the state was given arbitrary powers to appropriate land and capital nor where the threat of such development would occur. Though political and economic reforms started to take place in the Soviet Union during the 1980s, the domestic economic situation deteriorated to the point where the system was no longer sustainable due to the lack of any inflow of capital or technology.

E. The demise of the USSR from forces within was like the lifting of a floodgate for international foreign direct investment. According to United Nations statistics, the inflows and outflows of foreign direct investment in the world increased by six times from 1991 to 2000. In 1990, the majority of money flowing into developing economies came from international institutions such as the World Bank but by the middle of the decade about three-fourths came from private lenders. An increasing amount of money was also sent directly by individuals to their home family, accompanying the rise in migrants working and living in countries not of their birth. Though the legal frameworks regulating the flow of labor across borders is much less developed than that for goods/services and capital, the World Bank estimates that remittances sent to migrants' home countries increased from about $30 billion dollars in 1990 to about $300 billion dollars in 2006, a tenfold rise. Foreign direct investment and remittances are both largely positive aspects of an increasingly interconnected global finance system and help to create virtuous cycles of investment, growth, and saving. There were a series of events in the late 1990s, though, that revealed the full picture to be somewhat less rosy than extreme market exponents portrayed.

F. The Asian Financial Crisis of 1997 highlighted the drawbacks of the free flow of capital across different financial economies. A local currency crisis, which might have otherwise been contained in Asia in previous eras, spread from Thailand to the rest of Southeast Asia and then on to East Asia. With so much money pulled out of developing countries so quickly, the global investment community changed their outlook and investment patterns in the developing world, a change that still has knock-on effects to this day. The resulting calamity also involved the International Monetary Fund (IMF) as the institution that prescribed austerity measures had a chilling effect on its reputation and also made recipient countries very cynical of the true nature of their bailout packages and policy prescriptions.

G. In developing areas that seek greater access to capital, institutional policies which are mutually beneficial have to be devised and implemented. The complicated interplay of governments, international organizations, and private corporations belies any notion of quick fixes or 'one-size-fits-all' regulation. Other complicating factors, like the developed nations' need for labor in isolation, also need to be addressed, as it is not possible to deal with any one economic factor in isolation. Careful and measured policies can prevent economic bubbles from growing and popping. The rudimentary framework created vis-à-vis World Trade Organization rulings for goods and services has some potential to produce widespread economic growth. Until capital and labor can be adequately addressed likewise, the global economy will still be at risk from major shocks.
