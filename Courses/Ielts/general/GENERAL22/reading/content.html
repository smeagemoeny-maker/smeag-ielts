Buying or Selling a House: A Guide to Help You Through the Red Tape


Buying or selling a house can be a stressful time as you try to make your way through the paperwork. In reality, the process is quite simple.

The Basic Steps:
1. The seller (who is called the Vendor) prepares a document called the Vendor's Statement.
2. The seller advertises the house, receives offers from possible buyers, and accepts one of the offers.
3. The buyer (who is called the Purchaser) and seller sign a Contract of Sale, and the buyer pays a deposit, usually 10% of the sale price.
4. Both sides prepare for settlement, at which time the buyer pays the rest of the money owed on the house and the seller provides the documents needed to transfer ownership (from the seller to the buyer).
5. The buyer pays a tax called Stamp Duty and registers the change of ownership at the Land Registry.

What is Conveyancing?

Conveyancing is the legal term meaning the transfer of home ownership from one person to another. Proper conveyancing includes three essentials:
1. Investigation (before and after signing a Contract of Sale).
2. Negotiation.
3. Paperwork (legal documents).

Conveyancing for Sellers and buyers For sellers conveyancing starts before you even market the property with the preparation of a "Section 32 Vendor's Statement' and preferably a Contract of Sale as well.
Most buyers think conveyancing starts after you sign a Contract of Sale, but this idea is dangerous because if there is anything wrong it is too late to do anything about it.
Conveyancing Options
You can:
    ●  Do it yourself.
    ●  Pay a solicitor.
    ●  Pay a non-solicitor conveyancer.
Conveyancing Fees
Solicitor's fees vary from about $200 to $700, while non-solicitor conveyancers charge about $250 to $350. But watch out for hidden extras. An apparent low price may not include extras for phone calls, photocopying, storage or mark-ups on government charges, and may even include fictitious items. Dealing with banks is a normal procedure but some conveyancers even charge extra for that.
Doing It by Yourself
Paid conveyancing is also limited in value. Conveyancers work behind desks so they usually only do the paperwork. Often the important investigation and negotiation is left to their clients. For this reason there are real advantages to keeping personal control over conveyancing. To help people do this, a Conveyancing Kit sold for only $60 has been produced by Legal Kits of Victoria. It is suitable for buyers and sellers wishing to reduce costs and take control of their own conveyancing.

Westside Institute of Further Education

Information for new students

Vaccination requirements
In accordance with state law, all students must submit appropriate documentation proving immunity from measles, mumps and rubella. The following constitutes proof of immunity: two doses of measles vaccine and one dose each of mumps and rubella; blood tests indicating immunity to each disease or a disease history certified by a medical doctor (except for rubella). Students will be sent an Immunisation Record Declaration with their admission forms. While certain students may be exempt because of age, medical reasons, or religious belief, they must submit documentation of exemption before registration. Should a student’s vaccinations not be up to date, proof of an initial dose of measles, mumps and rubella vaccination must be submitted before registration. These students then have 30 days to provide evidence of their second dose of the measles vaccine to avoid expulsion from their course. Students failing to comply with these requirements will be excluded from classes and course fees will not be refunded.
Standard skill requirements
It is a requirement of the Institute that all students meet the standards of proficiency in the basic skills areas of mathematics, reading, speech and writing. These levels have been established by the Westside Institute Council and tests are administered by the Admissions Office prior to registration. Remedial instruction is offered to students who do not initially reach the level required. Attendance at these weekly two-hour sessions is compulsory until tests are passed successfully in all skill areas. Students enrolled in their final semester who are still unable to meet the competency requirements are unable to graduate.
Application deadlines
All applications require a completed application form and all supporting documents. The deadline for next semester is 20 March. Applications received after this date will be evaluated on a ‘first come, first served’ basis depending on availability of places. However, international students must have a completed application on file by 1 March to be considered for admission. Applications will not be complete until all required documents have been submitted. Official translations must be submitted for all documents written in a language other than English. Copies of the original documents must accompany translations.
Cancellation policy
A compulsory non-refundable enrolment fee is payable once only. Full refunds of tuition fees will be given if at least four weeks’ written notice is provided prior to commencement of the course. A cancellation charge equal to four weeks’ tuition will be charged if written notice of cancellation is received less than four weeks before the course begins. No refunds will be payable after a student has commenced studying at the school. If a student’s visa application has been denied, a full refund will be made by the Institute on receipt of a copy of the Embassy’s rejection letter.

Assignment Specifications

‘School of the Air’


New technology brings changes to remote education in Australia

A
Australia is a large country with a comparatively small population. Some Australians live hundreds of kilometres from their neighbours and maybe a thousand kilometres from the nearest town. Living in such remote areas can be tough and one of the main challenges families face is deciding how to educate their children. For older children and those students who want to enter tertiary level education, the best option is often to move to a city and attend boarding school or stay with relatives while they study. However, for young children it would not be appropriate to leave home and another solution would have to be found for isolated families. Over 50 years ago, a radio-based system was developed to bring education to the homes of thousands of Australian children in remote locations. More recently, advances in technology have brought substantial changes to this innovative system.

B
The HF (high frequency) radio transmitter was the basic means of course delivery for decades. In the beginning, the broadcasts were one way but when it became apparent that the students needed interaction a question and answer session was added after the teacher’s presentation. This innovation was followed by a range of initiatives as the radio schools tried to create learning opportunities similar to those in a typical classroom situation. As transport has improved in the last 50 years, home visits have become part of the curriculum. Teachers travel by air or road to their students’ homes to interact and give feedback to the students. The teachers themselves operate from a base and in some regions this operates as a school to which students can travel and attend for short periods of time. These times give students a chance to interact not only with teachers but also with their classmates.

C
The HF radio transmitter has been the backbone of communication since services commenced, but it can be incredibly unreliable. The radio signal often becomes distorted due to weather, and as the technology has aged, it has become difficult to find replacement parts. The most obvious drawback is the lack of visual clues students have as they try to learn by radio. There are many skills which are more effectively learned by following an example modeled by a teacher. For this reason, some schools introduced television broadcasts to supplement radio services. Despite the quality of the television programs, they were eventually discontinued due to their high cost.

D
The most recent phase of this technology is video lessons delivered by the Internet or satellite. Many teachers are eagerly embracing the technology as it allows them to demonstrate instead of relying on explanation. The other feature that is exciting for both staff and students is the interactive use of cameras by students at home. Using microphones, speakers, and cameras, up to two students and the teacher can talk at the same time. Students are able to clarify any doubts, share their work, and get feedback from the teacher. This learning environment seems like a vast improvement on the early days of crackling radio reception, but more research needs to be completed to determine if this technology is improving students' skills. Consequently, some states in Australia have taken the decision to wait until this cutting-edge technology has proven to be successful elsewhere before they adopt it themselves.

E
The trend to replace radio with video has not happened all over Australia. For some families, the cost of setting up video services proved prohibitively expensive, so in some areas the move has been to telephone instead of radio. Other telephone users said they also preferred to be able to participate from wherever there was a phone rather than having to be at a base. Another factor was the higher quality audio performance that is available via telephone, which is an important factor for students studying disciplines such as languages or music. For teachers, the telephone is also a very simple medium that allows them to cover a larger amount of material as they do not waste time sending or receiving material.

F
Whichever technology is chosen, it is important to remember that students actually spend very little time using it. Time spent communicating with teachers ranges from thirty minutes to a maximum of an hour a day as students get older. It is the person supervising the other five or six hours of the day who is often crucial to a student's success on the course. On average, only 30% of students have a private tutor hired by their family to supervise distance learning: the remaining 70% are supervised by their mothers and fathers, and for many of these parents, advances in technology have in fact done nothing to ease their burden.

G
The transition from radio to more recent forms of technology has been slower than one might expect. In some regions, a combination of different modes is used as infrastructure develops and people change their ways. An important point not to be forgotten is the fact that many people in isolated communities have a sentimental attachment to the radio because it has been such a vital part of their lives. Giving it up for good won't come easily to all.
